## Check the sections are complete before you touch the language

When the team sends my mom their sections, she doesn't start editing. She starts checking. Is each section in the right format? Is anything missing? Did the author stay within the page limit? Are the charts and graphs there? And is every claim backed by evidence, with a footnote pointing to where it came from?

It's tempting to skip this and start fixing sentences, because that feels like progress. But polishing a section that's missing half its footnotes is wasted work. The author will have to add them, and the new text will need editing again. Missing footnotes are also one of the mistakes she sees most often in final reports, so catching them on day one saves a lot of trouble at the end.

## Make every section start with its point

My mom asks every author to write in pyramid style. It's a common way of structuring reports, often linked to Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle: the main point comes first, then the arguments that support it, then the details. A reader who stops after the first sentence of a section should still know what it found.

Many people don't write that way. They build up to their conclusion, the way they did the research, so the finding sits at the bottom of the paragraph. Put five authors' sections next to each other and you get five different structures, on top of five different tones and five ideas of how long a section should be. Some people write very briefly. Many can't stop.

This is where most of her time goes. Rewriting sections into pyramid style, making sure the language and the message are easy to understand, removing repetition and cutting length. In a long report written by several people, repetition is one of the biggest problems.

## Protect what each author meant

Rewriting for structure and length means changing a lot of sentences, and every change is a chance to change the meaning. My mom handles that in three ways. She rereads everything she changed. When she isn't sure, she checks with the author directly. And she leaves comments in the document, sometimes with the wording she proposes, so the author can agree or push back.

She also uses AI to help reword sentences while keeping the message. That helps with the slow part, but the meaning still has to be checked by a person.

Then the authors review her edits and send back comments or corrections. She fixes those, and only after that does the report go to the external quality assurance expert. For a report that has been through the authors, her final fixing takes three to five days. The QA review takes one to two.

## The mistakes that survive to the final draft

I asked my mom which mistakes she sees in the final edit, her own or other people's. Her list:

- missing footnotes
- spelling and grammar errors
- numbering problems
- repetition, which gets worse the longer the report is
- run-on sentences
- edits that change the meaning of a message

Most of these are easy to fix and hard to find. In a report of around 100 pages, nobody spots a missing footnote or a broken number sequence by reading for meaning. One thing that helps is checking for them on purpose, one at a time, in a separate pass at the end: footnotes first, then numbering of sections, tables and figures, then spelling.

The last item on her list is different. A spelling error is visible to anyone. A changed meaning looks like a perfectly good sentence. The only reliable way to catch it is to keep every edit visible, so the author who wrote the original can see exactly what became of it.
